\chapter{Foundations and Related Work}\label{ch:foundations}

This chapter defines the concepts this thesis builds on and situates them in the
literature. \autoref{sec:parcellation} explains how a continuous brain scan becomes a
region-level graph. \autoref{sec:graph-learning} introduces graph representation learning
as applied to neuroimaging: message passing, attention, autoencoders and pooling.
\autoref{sec:longitudinal-modelling} extends this to sequences of visits, from
time-aware recurrent models to spatiotemporal graph transformers. \autoref{sec:open-problems}
closes the chapter by naming four open problems in this literature, each mapped to one of
the research questions stated in \autoref{sec:research-questions}.

\section{Brain Connectivity and Parcellation Schemes}\label{sec:parcellation}

\subsection{Parcellation and Atlas Choice}\label{sec:parcellation-atlas}

A resting-state functional \ac{MRI} scan measures a signal at every voxel in the brain,
far too many units to treat individually as nodes in a graph. Parcellation is the step
that reduces this to a manageable number of regions: a fixed atlas partitions the brain
volume or cortical surface into a finite set of regions, each a \ac{ROI}, and every
voxel's signal is subsequently summarised by the \ac{ROI} it falls into. Atlases fall into
two broad families.
Anatomical atlases place region boundaries at macroanatomical landmarks, identifiable on a
structural scan independently of any functional data; the Automated Anatomical Labelling
atlas, which divides the cortex and subcortex into ninety regions and is the atlas the
Brain-TokenGT competitor evaluated in this thesis uses \parencite{dong2023braintokengt}, is
of this kind. Functional atlases instead place boundaries where the intrinsic, resting-state
organisation of the cortex itself changes, on the reasoning that a region defined by a
shared functional signal is a more natural unit for a functional-connectivity analysis than
one defined by anatomy alone. The canonical seven-network partition of the cerebral cortex
into large-scale systems such as the default mode, sensorimotor and visual networks, derived
from the clustering structure of resting-state connectivity across a large cohort, is one
such functional atlas \parencite{yeo2011organization}. The Schaefer parcellation refines
this idea to a chosen number of finer-grained regions at that resolution by combining a local measure of
where the functional signal changes most sharply with a global measure of how well the
resulting regions align with the seven-network partition, and is offered at several
resolutions so that a given study can trade the number of regions against how much data
supports estimating each one \parencite{schaefer2018parcellation}. Because a cortical
atlas does not cover subcortical structures, a separate atlas is needed wherever those
structures are the object of analysis; one such atlas maps subcortical organisation using
functional-connectivity gradients rather than the anatomical boundaries a structural atlas
would use \parencite{tian2020subcortex}. \autoref{ch:data} states which atlas and which
resolution this thesis uses and why.

\subsection{Estimating Functional Connectivity}\label{sec:fc-estimation}

Once a scan is parcellated, every region contributes one time series, obtained by
averaging the blood-oxygen-level-dependent signal across the voxels the region contains.
Functional connectivity between two regions is a measure of how similarly their time
series behave. Pearson correlation between each pair of regional time series is the most
widely used such measure: it is symmetric, bounded, and directly interpretable as the
degree to which two regions' spontaneous activity rises and falls together. Applying it to
every pair of regions in a $|V|$-region atlas yields one $|V| \times |V|$ connectivity
matrix per scan, a dense, fully populated description of how strongly every region is
coupled to every other. Because a correlation coefficient is bounded and its sampling
variance depends on its own magnitude, this matrix is conventionally transformed before
any downstream analysis treats its entries as numerically comparable, so that a given
change in coupling strength is represented consistently regardless of where in the bounded
range it occurs. \autoref{ch:data} states the specific transform applied throughout this
thesis. Partial correlation and coherence are alternative similarity measures reported
elsewhere in this literature, controlling for the influence of other regions or capturing
frequency-specific coupling respectively, but the connectivity matrices this thesis works
with throughout are built from the same measure Pearson correlation provides.

\subsection{From a Connectivity Matrix to a Graph}\label{sec:graph-construction-background}

A dense connectivity matrix is not yet a graph in the sense a graph neural network
consumes: every region is connected to every other region with some non-zero weight, so
treating the matrix directly as an adjacency structure gives every node the same,
maximally dense neighbourhood, in which the specific pattern of which connections matter
is lost inside a uniform weighting. Converting a connectivity matrix into a graph
therefore requires a sparsification step that decides which entries become edges. Several
conventions exist. A proportional threshold retains a fixed fraction of the strongest
connections across the whole matrix; an absolute threshold retains every connection above
a fixed strength regardless of how many that leaves per region; a minimum spanning tree
keeps only the edges needed to keep the graph connected at minimum total edge weight; and a
per-region $k$-nearest-neighbour rule retains, for each region, its $k$ strongest
connections independently of how strong or weak the rest of the matrix is elsewhere. Each
convention encodes a different assumption about what should count as a meaningful
connection, and a benchmark study of graph neural networks for brain-network analysis
treats this construction step as a first-class design choice on the same footing as the
network architecture itself, rather than as a fixed preprocessing detail
\parencite{cui2023braingb}. \autoref{ch:methodology} states which of these conventions this
thesis applies and at what resolution.

\subsection{Node Features}\label{sec:node-features-background}

A graph neural network additionally requires a feature vector attached to every node, not
only an edge structure. Several conventions are used for a connectome graph. The
region's own row of the connectivity matrix, its full profile of pairwise coupling with
every other region, is one common choice, and has the property that a node's features and
the graph's own edges are built from the same underlying measurement rather than from two
independent modalities. Graph-theoretic summary statistics computed per region, such as
its degree or clustering coefficient, are a lower-dimensional alternative that discards the
pairwise profile in favour of a small number of topological descriptors. A fixed positional
or identity encoding, carrying no information about that scan's connectivity at all, is a
third option used when a model is meant to learn purely from graph structure. The
benchmark of \autoref{sec:graph-construction-background} catalogues this choice alongside
the edge-construction convention as a second design axis for the same reason
\parencite{cui2023braingb}.

\subsection{What a 200-Region Resolution Commits To}\label{sec:resolution-tradeoff}

Choosing how many regions an atlas divides the cortex into trades two properties against
each other. A finer parcellation captures functional boundaries at a smaller spatial scale,
following the same local-boundary rationale that motivates the Schaefer parcellation's own
construction \parencite{schaefer2018parcellation}, and gives a graph neural network more
nodes over which to represent that structure. The number of possible pairwise connections
grows quadratically with the number of regions, however, so a finer atlas also multiplies
the number of edges a sparsification rule must select from and the number of entries a
node's connectivity-profile feature vector carries. Against a labelled cohort that stays
fixed in size, a finer atlas therefore raises feature dimensionality without raising the
information available to estimate it from, which is exactly the small-sample difficulty
named in \autoref{sec:dl-neurodegeneration} and returned to as an open problem in
\autoref{sec:gap-evidence}. A coarser atlas, such as the ninety-region Automated Anatomical
Labelling atlas the Brain-TokenGT competitor uses \parencite{dong2023braintokengt}, sits at
the opposite end of this tradeoff, exchanging spatial specificity for a smaller feature
space relative to cohort size. \autoref{ch:data} and \autoref{ch:methodology} state which
resolution this thesis selects and \autoref{sec:capacity} returns to what that choice
implies once weighed against the trainable-parameter counts of the classifiers built on it.

\section{Graph Representation Learning in Neuroimaging}\label{sec:graph-learning}

\subsection{Message Passing on Connectomes}\label{sec:message-passing}

A graph neural network learns a representation for each node by repeatedly updating that
node's own feature vector with information drawn from its neighbours, a mechanism known as
message passing. In one layer of a graph convolutional network, a node's new
representation is a learned, weighted combination of its own current features and those of
every node it is connected to, so that after $L$ layers a node's representation reflects
information from every other node within $L$ hops of it in the graph
\parencite{kipf2017gcn}. Applied to a connectome graph, this lets a region's learned
representation depend not only on its own connectivity profile but on the profiles of the
regions it is connected to, which is the property that distinguishes a graph neural network
from a model that treats every region independently. An inductive variant of this idea
learns a fixed aggregation function that samples and combines a node's neighbourhood on the
fly, rather than a fixed set of per-node weights tied to one specific graph, which matters
for a connectome dataset in which every subject's graph has the same node set but a
subject-specific edge structure \parencite{hamilton2017graphsage}.

\subsection{Graph Attention}\label{sec:graph-attention}

The message-passing update of \autoref{sec:message-passing} treats every neighbour's
contribution according to a weight the graph structure alone determines, such as the
inverse of a node's degree. Not every connection is equally informative for a given task,
however, and a graph attention network instead learns a weight for each edge as a function
of the two nodes' own features, so that during training the network can learn to emphasise
some neighbours' contributions over others rather than treating the graph structure as
fixed evidence about how much attention each edge deserves \parencite{velickovic2018gat}.
A later analysis showed that the original formulation's attention weights do not depend on
the query node in the way the mechanism was intended to allow, and proposed a corrected
variant that restores this dependence \parencite{brody2022gatv2}; \autoref{sec:static-representation}
states where this corrected variant is used in this thesis's own encoder.

\subsection{Graph Autoencoders and the Reconstruction Pretext}\label{sec:graph-autoencoders-background}

A graph autoencoder learns a node-level or graph-level representation without using any
task label, by training an encoder to compress a graph into a lower-dimensional latent
representation and a decoder to reconstruct the original graph from that representation
alone; the reconstruction error is the only training signal. A variational graph
autoencoder extends this idea by having the encoder output a distribution over the latent
representation rather than a single point, regularised toward a fixed prior, and
reconstructing the graph's edge structure from a sample of that distribution through an
inner-product decoder \parencite{kipf2016vgae}. The appeal of this approach for a
neuroimaging setting with far more unlabelled scans than labelled subjects is that the
encoder can, in principle, be trained on every available scan regardless of whether that
scan carries a diagnostic label, and only afterward paired with a small classification head
trained on the labelled subset. \autoref{sec:static-representation} describes the
graph-attention and variational autoencoders this thesis builds on and extends, and
\autoref{sec:gap-ssl} states what remains open about whether this pretext objective
actually yields a representation a downstream classifier can use.

\subsection{Pooling Operators}\label{sec:pooling-background}

A classifier that predicts one label per subject, rather than one label per region, needs
a further step that reduces a graph's set of per-node representations to a single
graph-level vector. The simplest such operator is a fixed aggregation, such as taking the
elementwise mean or sum of every node's representation, which is permutation-invariant to
how the nodes happen to be ordered but treats every node as equally relevant to the final
prediction. An attention-weighted alternative instead learns a per-node relevance score and
pools nodes according to that learned weighting rather than uniformly; a sparse variant of
the function typically used to normalise such weights forces the least relevant nodes'
scores to exactly zero rather than merely small, which changes which nodes are excluded
from the pooled representation rather than only how much they contribute to it
\parencite{martins2016sparsemax}. A third family of pooling operators learns a hierarchical
cluster assignment for the graph's nodes and coarsens the graph layer by layer, producing a
sequence of progressively smaller graphs rather than reducing directly from every node to
one vector \parencite{ying2018diffpool}. \autoref{sec:static-representation} compares mean
and attention pooling directly for this thesis's own static classifiers.

\subsection{Contrastive Self-Supervision and the Foundation-Model Frontier}\label{sec:contrastive-and-foundation}

Reconstruction is not the only pretext task proposed for connectome data without labels.
A contrastive alternative instead trains an encoder so that two differently augmented
views of the same subject's connectivity graph are pulled together in representation space
while views drawn from different subjects are pushed apart, without ever reconstructing the
input matrix itself; this has been applied within a single cohort
\parencite{wang2023ucgl} and, using a learned graph-diffusion augmentation, across sites and
disorders \parencite{wang2025gcda}. At a substantially larger scale than any single-site
clinical cohort can reach, foundation models pretrained on tens of thousands of scans have
begun to establish what large-scale self-supervised pretraining can buy for downstream
diagnosis and prognosis tasks, predicting a representation of a masked portion of the input
directly in latent space rather than reconstructing it in input space
\parencite{dong2024brainjepa}. Whether any of this pretraining machinery, contrastive or
generative, contributes a representation that improves on the absence of a learned encoder
altogether is itself contested: a re-examination of graph deep learning against classical
machine learning across four large-scale neuroimaging studies found that the message-passing
mechanism central to a graph neural network does not help predictive performance as usually
assumed, and consistently degrades it \parencite{han2026rethinking}, and a separate study
found that graph transformers offer no advantage over conventional graph neural networks on
a standard connectome benchmark, with accuracy maintained even when every edge is removed
\parencite{lararangel2025limits}. Both studies evaluate cross-sectional cohorts at a scale
this thesis's single-site, longitudinal cohort does not reach; \autoref{sec:gap-ssl} states
how this thesis's own encoder ablation relates to their finding.

\section{Longitudinal and Spatiotemporal Modelling}\label{sec:longitudinal-modelling}

\subsection{Recurrent Models over Visit Sequences}\label{sec:recurrent-background}

A subject followed over several clinical visits contributes a sequence of representations,
one per visit, rather than a single one, and a recurrent neural network is the standard
architecture for consuming such a sequence: it maintains a hidden state that is updated at
every step by combining the current input with the state carried forward from the previous
step, so that the final hidden state, after every visit has been processed, is a
fixed-size summary of the whole sequence a classification head can act on. The long
short-term memory unit augments this basic recurrence with a set of learned gates that
control what is written to, kept in, and read from the hidden state at each step, which
addresses the tendency of a plain recurrent update to lose information from early in a long
sequence \parencite{hochreiter1997lstm}. A gated recurrent unit simplifies this gating
mechanism to fewer parameters while retaining the same basic property
\parencite{cho2014gru}. Both units assume, in their original form, that consecutive steps
of the sequence are equally spaced in time, an assumption clinical visit sequences do not
satisfy.

\subsection{Conditioning on Irregular Time}\label{sec:time-conditioning-background}

Clinical visits are not evenly spaced, and a recurrent unit that treats every step
identically regardless of the elapsed time since the last one discards information a
model could otherwise use. Outside the neuroimaging literature, this problem has been
addressed directly: a time-aware long short-term memory unit decays the carried-forward
cell state by an amount that depends on the elapsed time since the previous visit, so that
information from a distant past visit contributes less than information from a recent one
\parencite{baytas2017tlstm}, and a related architecture instead learns an attention
weighting over every prior visit, again as a function of elapsed time, so that the model
can attend selectively to earlier visits rather than relying only on how recently they
occurred \parencite{zhang2019attain}. Neither mechanism is specific to graph-structured
data; both were developed and evaluated on vectorised electronic health record sequences,
and this thesis claims no novelty for the mechanism of conditioning a recurrent update on
elapsed time. \autoref{ch:methodology} states the specific time-conditioning representation
this thesis's own recurrent classifiers use.

\subsection{Dynamic and Spatiotemporal Graph Networks}\label{sec:dynamic-graph-background}

A separate line of work keeps the graph structure of \autoref{sec:graph-learning}
explicit at every timestep rather than collapsing it to a fixed-size vector before any
temporal model is applied. One such architecture fuses a dynamic functional-connectivity
sequence with a separately estimated dynamic effective-connectivity sequence inside a
spatiotemporal graph neural network, using the directional effective-connectivity stream to
guide how the two are combined \parencite{chen2024knosys}. A second applies a
spatiotemporal graph convolutional network with a gradient-based class-activation mapping
head to a large, multi-site, cross-sectional cohort, reporting both the diagnostic
performance of the resulting model and an interpretability map of which functional networks
drive its predictions \parencite{zhang2024stgcgcam}. A third combines a dynamic
heterogeneous graph over multiple modalities, imaging, genetic and electronic-health-record
data among them, with a temporal-importance weighting loss that lets the model learn which
timepoints carry the most information for predicting both a continuous cognitive score and
a time-to-conversion outcome jointly \parencite{hassan2025magnetad}. These architectures
keep spatial and temporal information more tightly coupled than the pool-then-recur pattern
of \autoref{sec:recurrent-background}, but each couples them by applying a spatial graph
operation at every timestep before any temporal aggregation, rather than by letting a
temporal model see each region's own trajectory directly; \autoref{sec:gap-aggregation}
returns to what specifically remains open about this ordering.

\subsection{Spatiotemporal Graph Transformers and Brain-TokenGT}\label{sec:spatiotemporal-transformers-background}

A further architectural family replaces the recurrent core of
\autoref{sec:recurrent-background} with a transformer applied across both space and time
jointly. Brain TokenGT tokenises a subject's whole visit sequence at once: each region at
each timepoint, and each spatial and temporal edge connecting them, becomes a token with a
learned type identifier, and a transformer with orthogonal node identifiers reads out a
prediction from the full set of tokens rather than from a single terminal hidden state
\parencite{dong2023braintokengt}. Because it is evaluated on longitudinal, functional
connectome data with an explicit \ac{MCI}-to-\ac{AD} conversion task among its reported
results, it is the closest published match to the problem this thesis addresses, and its
publicly released implementation is audited directly as a comparator in this thesis, rather
than only cited. \autoref{ch:baselines} introduces this comparator in full and states how it
is evaluated under the protocol of \autoref{ch:methodology}.

\section{Open Problems Addressed in This Thesis}\label{sec:open-problems}

This section synthesises four open problems identified in the literature surveyed above,
each corresponding to one of the research questions framed in \autoref{sec:research-questions}.

\subsection{Where Spatial Aggregation Belongs in a Longitudinal Pipeline}\label{sec:gap-aggregation}

The recurrent and spatiotemporal architectures surveyed in
\autoref{sec:longitudinal-modelling} reduce spatial information to a fixed-size summary vector
before temporal modelling occurs. Time-aware recurrent mechanisms operate on vectorised
per-visit representations by construction, whereas dynamic and spatiotemporal graph networks
apply spatial message passing and graph pooling at each visit prior to temporal recurrence.
Consequently, region-specific trajectories across visits are collapsed into a global
subject-level summary before any temporal model processes them. Whether spatial pooling prior
to temporal modelling is necessary, or whether modelling regional trajectories directly before
spatial aggregation preserves disease-relevant signal, remains an open problem in the
literature. This gap motivates the investigation of architectural ordering framed in RQ3
(\autoref{sec:research-questions}).

\subsection{What Self-Supervision on Connectomes Learns}\label{sec:gap-ssl}

Reconstruction is the primary pretext task for connectome autoencoding
(\autoref{sec:graph-autoencoders-background}), operating under the assumption that recovering
connectivity matrices yields features informative for downstream classification. 
Recent audits across large cross-sectional cohorts demonstrate that graph message passing 
often fails to improve predictive performance over simpler tabular baselines \parencite{han2026rethinking} and that topological edges contribute
minimally to classification on standard benchmarks \parencite{lararangel2025limits}.
Nevertheless, these evaluations focus on cross-sectional datasets and do not isolate the
utility of the self-supervised pretext objective in longitudinal cohorts. Whether
self-supervised graph pretraining provides measurable discriminative utility in longitudinal
settings remains an open problem, corresponding to RQ2 (\autoref{sec:research-questions}).

\subsection{Evidence Standards Under Small Samples}\label{sec:gap-evidence}

In small-sample neuroimaging cohorts, empirical evaluation practices remain heterogeneous
across three distinct dimensions. First, candidate architectures are rarely evaluated against
pre-specified performance floors, such as static baselines or unregularised linear models
trained on identical splits. Second, published benchmarks frequently omit seed sensitivity
and replicate variance, obscuring whether reported gains exceed stochastic variation
\parencite{cui2023braingb}. Third, longitudinal cohort protocols vary substantially in visit
filtering, handling of variable sequence lengths, and split definitions, creating opportunities
for data leakage that inflate reported classification accuracy
\parencite{shim2021leakage,yagis2021leakage,rosenblatt2024leakage}. Because these factors
confound cross-study comparisons, establishing whether longitudinal architectures provide
genuine gains over static representations requires standardized baseline evaluation, motivating
RQ1 (\autoref{sec:research-questions}).

\subsection{Time and Cohort as Confounds}\label{sec:gap-confounds}

Aggregating neuroimaging data across multiple cohorts is a common strategy to increase
sample size, but multi-cohort pooling introduces systematic confounds. First, observational
follow-up duration often correlates with conversion outcomes, as extended tracking windows
increase the probability of observing conversion events. Second, visit-time semantics differ
across studies; equating nominal protocol visits with continuous elapsed time since baseline
introduces temporal misalignment. Third, pooling across cohorts introduces cohort identity
as an unpenalised nuisance variable that neural representations can encode. Consequently,
whether models trained on multi-cohort aggregations learn generalisable pathological features
or rely on site-specific acquisition properties remains an open question, motivating RQ4
(\autoref{sec:research-questions}).

\bigskip

Addressing these four open problems requires systematic experimental formulation.
\autoref{ch:data} describes the clinical cohorts and longitudinal quality control procedures,
while \autoref{ch:methodology} establishes the models and evaluation frameworks designed to
interrogate each gap.
