\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}

This chapter states the clinical decision this thesis addresses, positions resting-state
functional \ac{MRI} against the other modalities available for that decision, surveys what
deep learning has contributed to neurodegenerative-disease prediction together with the
difficulties that recur across that literature, and closes with the research questions
this thesis answers, the contributions it makes toward answering them, and how the
remaining chapters are organised.

\section{Clinical Motivation and the Diagnostic Paradigm Shift}\label{sec:motivation}

\Ac{MCI} denotes a stage of measurable cognitive decline that does not yet impair a
patient's activities of daily living \parencite{petersen2004mci}. It sits between normal
cognitive ageing and dementia, and a diagnosis of \ac{MCI} carries no guarantee about what
follows: within any given observation window, some \ac{MCI} patients progress to \ac{AD},
some remain cognitively stable, and some revert toward normal cognition
\parencite{petersen2004mci}. This heterogeneity is what makes \ac{MCI} the decision point
of interest for a predictive model, rather than the point of diagnosis itself. A diagnosis
of \ac{AD} dementia confirms a process that is already established and, at present,
largely irreversible; the clinically actionable question is instead whether a patient
currently diagnosed with \ac{MCI} will convert to \ac{AD}, since that is the point at which
a change in monitoring frequency, an earlier referral, or a patient's own planning can
still be acted on.

The diagnostic framework this thesis inherits treats \ac{AD} as a biological process
defined by its underlying pathology rather than by the clinical syndrome it eventually
produces \parencite{jack2018niaaa}. Under this framework, cognitive symptoms are a late and
variable readout of a process that can, in principle, be tracked through biomarkers years
before a clinical diagnosis is made. The conversion label used as the prediction target
throughout this thesis is a clinical operationalisation of that shift: a subject is
labelled a converter if a later visit confirms a transition from \ac{MCI} to \ac{AD}, and
labelled stable \ac{MCI} otherwise. \autoref{ch:data} describes how this label is
constructed for each cohort used here.

Whether a given subject is labelled a converter or stable depends on how long that subject
is followed after their \ac{MCI} diagnosis. Extending the observation window increases the
chance that a true, eventual conversion is captured before a label is assigned, which
lowers label noise; the same extension shrinks the pool of subjects who have already
completed the full window by the time a cohort is assembled, which lowers the usable
cohort size. No choice of window removes this tradeoff, and it is resolved differently
across the cohorts this thesis draws on. \autoref{ch:data} states which choice each cohort
makes and why.

The primary scope of this thesis is the methodological evaluation of supervised
longitudinal classification approaches for \ac{MCI}-to-\ac{AD} conversion. It does not
claim to develop or validate a normative healthy-reference framework for individualized
abnormality detection.

\section{Resting-State Functional MRI as a Non-Invasive Window}\label{sec:rsfmri}

Three other modalities are established for tracking \ac{AD} pathology, and each carries a
cost that resting-state \ac{fMRI} does not. Measuring \ac{CSF} biomarkers of amyloid and
tau requires a lumbar puncture, an invasive procedure that limits how often and how widely
it can be repeated in a longitudinal cohort. Imaging amyloid or tau load with \ac{PET}
requires a radiotracer injection and a dedicated scanner, which raises cost and restricts
availability outside specialised centres. Structural \ac{MRI} is non-invasive and widely
available, but it detects atrophy, a late, largely irreversible marker that becomes
measurable only after substantial neuronal loss has already occurred.

Resting-state \ac{fMRI} measures spontaneous low-frequency fluctuations in the
blood-oxygen-level-dependent signal while a subject lies at rest, requiring no radiotracer,
no invasive sampling, and no task the subject must perform correctly to be useful. Because
\ac{AD} pathology is understood to disrupt functional connectivity between brain regions
before structural degradation is visible on a T1-weighted scan, resting-state \ac{fMRI}
offers a window onto network-level change at a stage before atrophy-based measures become
informative. This sensitivity is not confined to \ac{AD}: functional-connectivity
alterations detected with resting-state \ac{fMRI} extend into brain tissue distant from a
visible lesion and correlate with independently measured markers of disease severity in
other neurological conditions, and the sequence itself adds no meaningful burden to a
routine clinical scanning protocol \parencite{stoecklein2020glioma}. The same properties
that make resting-state \ac{fMRI} attractive for tracking network disruption in general
motivate its use here specifically for the \ac{MCI}-to-\ac{AD} conversion question:
it is repeatable across a longitudinal cohort, it does not require an invasive procedure or
a radiotracer, and it is sensitive to the connectivity change the disease model of
\autoref{sec:motivation} places before symptom onset. A subject's diagnostic classification
against this connectivity-change framework is itself defined jointly with cognitive and
fluid-biomarker criteria, staged along a continuum from asymptomatic biomarker positivity
through subjective cognitive decline to \ac{MCI} and dementia
\parencite{jessen2023scd}; \ac{fMRI}-derived connectivity is one candidate marker within
that staged framework, evaluated on its own throughout this thesis rather than fused with
the cognitive or fluid measures that define the clinical stage.

\section{Deep Learning in Neurodegenerative Disease}\label{sec:dl-neurodegeneration}

Applying deep learning to functional-connectivity data for \ac{MCI}-to-\ac{AD} prediction
has moved through several architectural families in a short span of years. Classifiers
combining a convolutional feature extractor with a recurrent layer over successive visits
report high in-sample discrimination between progressive and stable \ac{MCI}. Time-aware
recurrent networks developed outside the neuroimaging literature address a closely related
problem directly: standard recurrent units assume a constant elapsed time between steps,
which does not hold for irregularly spaced clinical visits, and both a time-decayed cell
state \parencite{baytas2017tlstm} and an attention mechanism over prior visits weighted by
elapsed time \parencite{zhang2019attain} have been proposed to correct for it. Graph neural
networks that treat each brain region as a node and each functional connection as an edge
have been proposed as a more direct fit to connectome data than a convolutional or purely
recurrent model, on the reasoning that functional connections encode topological rather
than spatial relationships, and a dedicated benchmark now exists to standardise how such
graph models are built and compared \parencite{cui2023braingb}. Self-supervised
pretraining, contrasting differently augmented views of the same connectivity graph before
any label is used, has been proposed as a way to make effective use of unlabelled scans
across sites and disorders \parencite{wang2023ucgl,wang2025gcda}, and architectures that
explicitly fuse dynamic functional and effective connectivity have been proposed for the
same disorder-classification setting \parencite{chen2024knosys}. At the other end of the
scale, foundation models pretrained on tens of thousands of scans have begun to establish
what large-scale pretraining can buy for downstream diagnosis and prognosis tasks
\parencite{dong2024brainjepa}, a regime unavailable to a single-site cohort of the size
used in this thesis. Cost-sensitive objectives have separately been proposed to address the
class imbalance inherent to staging tasks in which most patients do not progress within
the observed window \parencite{hu2024cswcl}. Time-to-event models that predict a
continuous risk of conversion rather than only its binary outcome have also been applied
directly to functional-connectome data \parencite{kim2024prognostic}.

Reported accuracies across this literature are not directly comparable, and this thesis
does not treat them as a benchmark to be matched or exceeded. Studies differ in cohort,
conversion window, parcellation, and validation scheme, and, independently of any of
those choices, several forms of data leakage have been shown to inflate reported
performance well beyond what a leakage-free protocol would support: feature selection
performed before rather than within cross-validation inflates accuracy on neuropsychiatric
biomarker tasks \parencite{shim2021leakage}, splitting three-dimensional scans at the
slice level rather than the subject level inflates test accuracy by 30 to 55 percentage
points on \ac{AD} and Parkinson's disease classification \parencite{yagis2021leakage}, and
a systematic audit across four large connectome datasets finds that feature and subject
leakage measurably inflate prediction performance in exactly this class of model
\parencite{rosenblatt2024leakage}. Because the leakage status of a given published pipeline
is often not verifiable from its report, this thesis competes on evaluation protocol
rather than on a published accuracy figure, and does not place its own results in a table
beside them.

Three difficulties recur across this literature regardless of architecture. First, the
number of usable functional-connectivity edges grows quadratically with the number of
parcellated brain regions, while the number of labelled subjects in a single-site
longitudinal cohort remains in the low hundreds at best; a model with enough capacity to
fit that many features is correspondingly prone to fitting subject-specific noise rather
than disease-relevant signal. Second, visits in a longitudinal cohort occur at uneven
intervals with missing follow-ups, producing variable-length sequences that force a
choice between padding, masking, or a time-aware modelling mechanism, and no standard
protocol for evaluating longitudinal models under this irregularity has been established.
Third, cohorts assembled from multiple sites differ in scanner field strength and
acquisition protocol in ways that a single-site cohort does not, introducing site identity
as a nuisance variable a model can inadvertently learn to exploit. \autoref{ch:foundations}
treats each of these three difficulties in detail and states the specific open problem this
thesis addresses within each.

\section{Research Questions, Objectives and Contributions}\label{sec:research-questions}

This thesis is a methodological evaluation of graph-based longitudinal classification
approaches for \ac{MCI}-to-\ac{AD} conversion, including cross-cohort transfer and
reproducibility analyses. It is organised around four research questions, stated here and
answered in \autoref{ch:results}.

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1] Does longitudinal modelling add information beyond static \ac{FC}
    representations? Answered in \autoref{sec:rq1}.
  \item[RQ2] Does spatial-first graph representation learning add value?
    Answered in \autoref{sec:rq2}.
  \item[RQ3] Does temporal-first modelling improve over spatial-first modelling?
    Answered in \autoref{sec:rq3}.
  \item[RQ4] Do the findings transfer to an independent cohort?
    Answered in \autoref{sec:rq4}.
\end{description}

Answering these questions builds on a pre-existing research programme: subject-level
\ac{FC} graph modelling, a graph-attention autoencoder for static representation learning,
and a recurrent trajectory classifier were already part of the project framework this
thesis extends. \autoref{sec:preexisting} states this framework and its provenance in
full. Within that framework, the contributions delivered by this thesis are the following.

\begin{description}
  \item[Temporal-first trajectory modelling.] Every longitudinal classifier inherited into
    this project pools a visit's connectome graph to a single vector before the temporal
    model sees it. This thesis inverts that order: each region's own temporal connectivity
    trajectory is modelled first with a node-shared recurrent network without graph propagation
    or autoencoding, and the graph is pooled spatially only afterward. The architecture (S1),
    its implementation, and the ablation sequence that tests it against the inherited spatial-first
    stack are thesis work, described in \autoref{sec:tfgn} and evaluated in \autoref{sec:rq3}.
  \item[Capacity accounting against the subject-level denominator.] An explicit analysis
    of trainable parameters per subject, rather than per scan, motivating why a smaller
    recurrent head was tested against the inherited spatial-first configuration.
    \autoref{sec:capacity} interprets the resulting pattern.
  \item[Characterising what reconstruction pretraining learns on this cohort size.] A
    statistical comparison of within-cohort and between-cohort connectivity variance,
    together with a scale-free reconstruction-fidelity measurement applied to both the
    inherited graph-attention autoencoder and a variational encoder built during this
    thesis, motivating the move from an encoder-centred evaluation to the embed-then-classify
    comparison of \autoref{sec:rq2}. \autoref{sec:static-representation} and
    \autoref{sec:ssl-contribution} report and interpret this respectively.
  \item[A variational encoder with a non-standard free-bits objective.] Two variants of a
    variational graph autoencoder, each with a hard-clamp-plus-quadratic-shortfall
    free-bits loss designed to avoid the posterior collapse that a plain clamp or a
    softplus relaxation produce. Described in \autoref{sec:static-representation} and
    \autoref{ch:app-implementation}.
  \item[A time-conditioned recurrent classification head.] A recurrent classifier
    consuming a sequence of per-visit connectome embeddings, with gate updates conditioned
    on the inter-visit interval, described in \autoref{sec:spatial-first} and serving
    throughout \autoref{ch:results} as the spatial-first comparator against which
    temporal-first modelling is tested.
  \item[Latent-space and interpretability studies.] A linear conversion axis extracted from
    the classifier's decision boundary, a latent-steering validation that decodes points
    along that axis back to connectivity space, and a per-dimension and per-region
    separability analysis of the latent representation, reported in
    \autoref{sec:interpretability-results}.
  \item[Perturbation and robustness testing.] Four perturbation methods, each targeting a
    distinct failure mode, together with a metadata-calibrated simulation of cross-cohort
    scanner drift, reported in \autoref{sec:robustness-results}.
  \item[Classical statistical characterisation before any model is fitted.] A
    converter-versus-non-converter comparison of the connectivity matrices using
    permutational multivariate analysis of variance, the network-based statistic, and
    per-edge effect sizes under false-discovery-rate control, establishing whether a group
    difference exists at all before a classifier is asked to find one.
    \autoref{ch:data} and \autoref{sec:capacity} draw on this characterisation.
\end{description}

\section{Thesis Organisation}\label{sec:organisation}

\autoref{ch:foundations} defines the concepts this thesis builds on: parcellation and
connectivity graph construction, graph representation learning as applied to
neuroimaging, longitudinal and spatiotemporal modelling, and the four open problems
introduced in \autoref{sec:dl-neurodegeneration} that the research questions of this
chapter address. \autoref{ch:data} describes the cohorts used, how the conversion label
and the visit-timing representation are constructed for each, and the preprocessing
pipeline they pass through. \autoref{ch:methodology} states the pre-existing framework
this thesis extends, the graph construction and static representation-learning stage, the
spatial-first and temporal-first trajectory models compared throughout, and the
experimental protocol that governs every subsequent evaluation. \autoref{ch:baselines}
introduces the published comparator audited against that protocol. \autoref{ch:results}
reports what happened under the protocol of \autoref{ch:methodology}, organised by the
four research questions of \autoref{sec:research-questions}, together with the
interpretability and robustness results. \autoref{ch:discussion} interprets those results
against the scope stated in \autoref{sec:motivation} and states the limitations of the
evaluated protocol. \autoref{ch:conclusion} summarises the answers to the four research
questions and states the work this thesis leaves open. The appendices collect
mathematical derivations, implementation detail, the reproducibility and cross-validation
tables, and the software and hardware environment, kept out of the main narrative so that
it states only what is necessary to understand or reproduce the method.
