\section{Scope of the Claims}\label{sec:scope-of-claims}

In this chapter we interpret the results reported in \autoref{ch:results}. We state what
the evaluated protocol licenses and what it does not, and we do not restate the numbers
of \autoref{ch:results} beyond what a given interpretation requires.

The primary scope of this thesis is the methodological evaluation of supervised
longitudinal classification approaches for \ac{MCI}-to-\ac{AD} conversion. It does not
claim to develop or validate a normative healthy-reference framework for individualized
abnormality detection.

The protocol licenses an in-domain comparison of longitudinal architectures on a pooled
\ac{ADNI}-and-\ac{DELCODE} cohort of $n=248$ subjects, evaluated out-of-fold across four
seeds, together with a single one-shot read of a held-out in-domain test set and an
external \ac{OASIS} cohort for the selected configuration and its pre-specified
secondaries. It does not license a claim of external validation: \ac{OASIS} is treated as
a transfer target rather than as a held-out sample from the training distribution, and
\autoref{sec:cohort-dependence} states what the pooled protocol's own diagnostics say about
that distinction. Every keep-or-drop decision in \autoref{ch:results} is a screening
heuristic at four seeds rather than a significance test, so an architectural comparison
that this thesis reports as inconclusive is not thereby shown to be false; it is reported
as undetectable at this sample size, which is a narrower claim.

The protocol does not license a time-to-event claim. Predicting the interval to conversion,
rather than only its binary outcome, would require a survival model fit to the same
cohorts under the same leakage controls used throughout \autoref{ch:methodology}; this
extension was scoped out of the present evaluation and is named again as future work in
\autoref{ch:conclusion}. The protocol also does not license a claim about a
healthy-reference or individualized-deviation framework: every classifier compared in
\autoref{ch:results} is trained directly on the conversion label, not on a divergence from
a normative population, and the pre-existing framework this thesis builds on, described in
\autoref{sec:preexisting}, is attributed to the broader research programme rather than to
the present work.

\section{Capacity and Inductive Bias Under Sample Constraint}\label{sec:capacity}

In this section we interpret what the parameter counts of \autoref{sec:rq2} and
\autoref{sec:rq3} imply about the cohort size available to this thesis, drawing on four
comparisons that were run independently of one another and that all point in the same
direction.

The first comparison is the \ac{DELCODE}-only encoder ablation of \autoref{sec:rq2}: the
configuration with no graph encoder at all scores within fold-to-fold spread of the frozen
pretrained configuration on cross-validation \ac{AUC}, while using approximately
seventeen times fewer trainable parameters. The second is the pooled temporal-first
ablation of \autoref{sec:rq3}: the selected configuration exceeds the randomly initialised
spatial-first floor by 0.186 \ac{AUC} at fourteen times fewer parameters, and exceeds the
pretrained spatial-first floor by 0.030 \ac{AUC} at 7.6 times fewer trainable parameters.
The third comparison runs in the opposite direction and reaches the same conclusion: two
configurations that add parameters on top of the selected configuration, a temporal
saliency gate and a dual-score head, both score lower than the configuration they extend.
The fourth comparison is external to this thesis's own architectures: the published
competitor evaluated in \autoref{sec:braintokengt} processes on the order of one thousand
four hundred tokens per subject through six hundred thousand trainable parameters, against
roughly eighty training subjects per fold in the matched-window comparison of that section.
On the pooled protocol it scores below every configuration in the temporal-first ablation
sequence that exceeds Tier 1.

Four independent comparisons that each add capacity find no corresponding gain, and one
that removes capacity relative to two established baselines finds a gain instead. This
pattern is consistent with the sample size, rather than the capacity of any candidate
architecture, being the binding constraint on classification performance at this cohort
size. It does not follow from these comparisons alone that a larger architecture could not
help on a larger cohort; the claim is scoped to the sample sizes evaluated here.

A statistical characterisation of the underlying connectivity matrices, reported in
\autoref{sec:group-stats}, offers a plausible account of why this pattern holds. The ratio
of within-group to between-group edge variance for the converter-versus-non-converter
contrast is on the order of 0.016 under every correlation measure tested, and no single
edge among the 5{,}995 examined reaches a ratio favouring the between-group component. A
model with more free parameters is not thereby better positioned to separate two groups
whose edge-level variation is dominated by within-group heterogeneity; additional capacity
gives such a model more ways to fit that heterogeneity rather than more ways to isolate the
smaller between-group signal. This is offered as a plausible explanation for the observed
capacity pattern, not as a demonstration, since no experiment in this thesis directly
manipulates the variance ratio and observes its effect on classification performance.

\section{What Self-Supervised Pretraining Contributed}\label{sec:ssl-contribution}

In this section we interpret the reconstruction-pretraining results of \autoref{sec:rq2}
and \autoref{sec:rq3} against the value that self-supervised pretraining is claimed to add
in prior work on this modelling framework, described in \autoref{sec:static-representation}.
This question is asked under both the \ac{DELCODE}-only protocol of \autoref{sec:rq2} and
the pooled protocol of \autoref{sec:rq3}.

Two comparisons in \autoref{sec:rq2} together bound what reconstruction pretraining
contributes. The frozen pretrained encoder outperforms the randomly initialised encoder
trained end to end on cross-validation \ac{AUC} by a wide margin, and its spread across
seeds is smaller, so reconstruction pretraining stabilises optimisation relative to a
random start. At the same time, the configuration with no graph encoder at all also
outperforms the randomly initialised encoder, and does so at seventeen times fewer
trainable parameters than the frozen pretrained configuration itself. A representation
that is beaten by the absence of any learned encoder is not thereby shown to transfer
useful structure to the downstream task; the two comparisons together are consistent with
pretraining serving as a stabiliser of a difficult optimisation problem rather than as a
source of task-relevant representation, and \autoref{sec:rq2} does not distinguish between
these two roles by itself.

The reconstruction-fidelity measurement of \autoref{sec:rq2} constrains what a pretrained
encoder's representation could plausibly contain in the first place. The pretrained
encoder's feature reconstruction reaches a median Pearson correlation of approximately
0.05 against its own held-out input, which falls in the band this thesis's own convention
labels poor, and its adjacency reconstruction is negative. An encoder whose decoder cannot
reconstruct the input it was trained to reconstruct has correspondingly little basis for
having captured a representation that a frozen downstream head could exploit, and this is
consistent with, rather than contradicted by, the encoder ablation result above.

On the pooled protocol of \autoref{sec:rq3}, the same question receives two further
answers that diverge from one another. Initialising the node-shared recurrent core from a
self-supervised forecasting checkpoint yields a small gain that the fold-matched statistic
treats as real and the pooled statistic treats as noise; the pre-specified one-standard-error
tie-breaker resolves the disagreement in favour of the simpler configuration without a
pretrained initialisation, so this contrast does not establish that node-level forecasting
pretraining transfers value at this cohort size, only that the two available statistics do
not agree strongly enough to justify the added component. A second, unrelated pretraining
signal, an auxiliary reconstruction stage attached to the selected configuration, collapses
classification performance substantially. Because the matched comparator for this
contrast under a self-supervised encoder on both sides also failed to train usefully, the
pre-specified pooling-order contrast that this second attempt was meant to answer remains
untested rather than answered in the negative, a point \autoref{sec:limitations} returns to.

The fine-tuning configuration of \autoref{sec:rq2}, in which the pretrained encoder is unfrozen and
trained jointly with the classification head, shows a further property of this pretraining
regime: its held-out test performance varies sharply across seeds, and this variation is
bit-reproducible rather than a symptom of run-to-run execution noise, which
\autoref{sec:limitations} discusses further as a reproducibility question in its own right.
Three non-exclusive mechanisms are consistent with this instability, ranked here by how
directly the observed configuration bears on them. The first is a shared learning rate
across the unfrozen encoder and the freshly initialised classification head: the encoder
receives the same step size as an untrained head, so early updates driven by that head can
overwrite the pretrained representation. The second is the capacity-to-sample ratio once
the encoder is unfrozen, at approximately $966{,}000$ trainable parameters against roughly
$106$ training subjects per fold. The third is a mismatch between the reconstruction
objective the encoder was pretrained on and the classification objective it is fine-tuned
on. No experiment in this ablation sequence isolates any one of these three from the other
two, since the configuration that would test the leading candidate directly, a
discriminatively scaled learning rate for the unfrozen encoder, was not run before the
writing deadline. We therefore report them as ranked candidates rather than as an
established cause.

A qualitative finding reported in \autoref{sec:rq2} offers a mechanism for why a
reconstruction objective struggles to contribute at this cohort size: a statistical
characterisation of the raw connectivity matrices found that within-cohort between-patient
variation exceeds between-cohort variation by a wide margin, so a reconstruction objective
evaluated at this sample size is consistent with spending its representational capacity on
subject-specific idiosyncrasy rather than on the disease-relevant signal a downstream
classifier needs. This account is offered as a plausible explanation rather than as an
isolated demonstration, since no experiment in this thesis holds subject idiosyncrasy fixed
while varying the reconstruction objective. A closely related question, whether the
message-passing step of a graph encoder adds value at all under this pooling order, has
also been reported at larger scale in the literature; the encoder-ablation finding of
\autoref{sec:rq2} is best read as an extension of that question into a smaller-sample,
longitudinal setting with the pretext objective itself ablated, rather than as an
independent discovery of the same result.

\section{Cohort Dependence and the Limits of Transfer}\label{sec:cohort-dependence}

In this section we interpret the per-cohort results, the cohort-identity probe and the
gradient-reversal mitigation reported in \autoref{sec:rq4}, and what together they say
about whether the representation learned under the pooled protocol is cohort-independent.

The per-cohort breakdown of \autoref{sec:rq4} shows that in-domain performance differs
substantially between the two pooled cohorts for every configuration evaluated, and that
this gap is widest for the spatial-first configurations: the frozen pretrained
spatial-first configuration scores near chance on \ac{ADNI} while scoring strongly on
\ac{DELCODE}, so its single pooled figure describes neither cohort on its own. The
cohort-identity probe adds a second, independent line of evidence: a linear classifier
recovers cohort of origin from the pooled patient representation at approximately 0.86
\ac{AUC} for every full-pool configuration, well above the pre-specified escalation
threshold of 0.75, despite no cohort label ever entering the training objective. Taken
together, a representation from which cohort of origin is this strongly decodable, and
whose in-domain performance differs this much between the two cohorts it pools, is
consistent with a representation that remains strongly cohort-dependent rather than one
that has been driven toward a shared, cohort-invariant encoding by the pooled training
objective alone.

The external read on \ac{OASIS} is where this cohort dependence is tested directly, and it
is at chance for every configuration in the one-shot evaluation of \autoref{sec:rq4}: the
selected configuration and its two pre-specified secondaries all fall within one standard
error of an \ac{AUC} of 0.5 at this sample size, against an in-domain test \ac{AUC} in the
high 0.7s for the same three configurations. We report this as no evidence of transfer to
an unseen cohort, not as evidence that the learned representation actively fails on it,
since the observed scores do not fall below the noise floor around chance. The strong
cohort-identity decodability reported above is consistent with cohort-specific structure in
the representation contributing to this absence of transfer, since \ac{OASIS} is exactly
the category of unseen cohort such a dependence would be expected to fail on; we do not
draw a causal conclusion from the probe result alone, because no experiment in this
ablation sequence isolates the probe's contribution from the many other differences between
the cohorts, among them site, acquisition protocol and inter-visit interval structure, that
\autoref{sec:limitations} discusses separately. We equally draw no conclusion from these
results about which architecture evaluated in this thesis, or the published competitor
characterised in \autoref{sec:braintokengt}, would transfer better to an external cohort;
\autoref{sec:comparability} states directly that the comparison between them is not
constructed to answer that question, and nothing in this chapter revisits that boundary.

The pre-specified gradient-reversal mitigation, attached to the same pooled representation
the probe scores and run once the escalation threshold was found to be exceeded, moved
performance on both axes it targeted in the direction opposite to its design intent: pooled
out-of-fold \ac{AUC} decreased and cohort-identity decodability increased. This outcome
does not identify cohort-invariant representation learning under this pooling protocol as
an unsolvable problem; it identifies this particular mitigation, as configured, as not
having solved it. The candidate explanation recorded in \autoref{sec:rq4}, that the
reversed gradient is unweighted relative to the classification loss, is a hypothesis rather
than a finding, since the comparison of gradient magnitudes between the two objectives that
would test it directly was not run.

A separate observation concerns the pre-specified escalation trigger itself as a process
rather than as a result. The threshold of 0.75 had been exceeded from the first configuration run
onward, and this was not read against the pre-specified rule until the tables of
\autoref{sec:rq4} were assembled together at the end of the ablation sequence. The
underlying quantity, the cohort-probe value for each configuration, was computed and
persisted correctly at every step, so no measurement was lost; what was missing was the act
of checking a computed value against its own rule at the point the rule was meant to fire.
Every in-domain out-of-fold result reported in \autoref{ch:results} is unaffected by this
gap, since those quantities do not depend on cross-cohort transfer and were not themselves
selected using the probe. We record this as evidence that a pre-specified check is only as
protective as the discipline of reading it at the moment it applies, a property of process
that a quantity's own correctness does not by itself guarantee.

The simulated scanner-drift sweep of \autoref{sec:robustness-results}, evaluated on a
different architecture and a single seed, offers an independent observation consistent with
the cohort-dependence account above without adding to it as evidence: at acquisition
variation calibrated to the two external cohorts' own measured heterogeneity, classification
sensitivity for the converter class falls toward zero as predictions revert to the majority
stable-\ac{MCI} class. Because this is a metadata-calibrated simulation on synthetic
perturbations rather than an evaluation against the real external connectivity matrices
used in \autoref{sec:rq4}, and because it evaluates a different recurrent architecture at a
single seed, it is offered here only as a second, independently obtained signal pointing in
the same direction as the \ac{OASIS} result, not as corroborating evidence in the technical
sense.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

In this section we discuss five limitations of the results reported in
\autoref{ch:results}: cohort size, the conversion label as a censored observation,
informative attrition, single-site development, and the limits of a negative result.

\paragraph{Cohort size.} At the in-domain test size of 64 subjects and the external size of
60 subjects, the resolvability statement of \autoref{sec:protocol} puts the noise floor on
an \ac{AUC} difference at approximately 0.08, and on the larger 248-subject out-of-fold pool
at approximately 0.04. Every keep-or-drop verdict in \autoref{sec:rq3} is therefore a
screening heuristic at four seeds, not a significance test, and the asymmetry this implies
matters for how the results are read: a configuration that fails to clear its comparator is
reported as undetectable at this sample size, never as shown to be harmful, while a
configuration that clears it is reported as worth carrying forward, never as proven
superior. Cohort size is the constraint underlying most of the remaining limitations in this
section, since a larger cohort would narrow the resolvability floor that makes several of
them binding in the first place.

\paragraph{The conversion label as a censored observation.} Converter status is assigned
from a fixed follow-up window, described in \autoref{ch:data}, rather than from the exact
clinical date of conversion, so a subject observed for a shorter window could in principle
convert later than the window captures. Widening the window would reduce this censoring at
the cost of a smaller cohort, since fewer subjects have sufficiently long follow-up to
qualify, so the window length in \autoref{ch:data} reflects a trade-off between cohort size
and label precision rather than a setting free of consequence. \ac{OASIS} carries a further,
more direct form of this limitation: nine subjects in the external cohort have their conversion
label unresolved at the time of the evaluation reported in \autoref{sec:rq4}.

\paragraph{Informative attrition.} Converters and stable \ac{MCI} subjects do not have the
same average number of visits available in the \ac{DELCODE} held-out split: converters
average more scans per subject than stable subjects do. A visit count that correlates with
the label is a route by which a classifier could learn to exploit visit count itself rather
than the trajectory information the visit count carries, an alternative explanation
\autoref{ch:results} guards against in two ways: the static single-visit probe reported
alongside every configuration in \autoref{sec:rq1} and \autoref{sec:rq3} sits at chance for
every one of them, and the Tier-3 robustness veto of \autoref{sec:protocol} would fail a
configuration whose score correlates too strongly with scan count within stable subjects.
Neither control eliminates the underlying attrition pattern in the cohort; both bound how
much of the reported signal that attrition pattern could plausibly explain.

\paragraph{Single-site development.} \ac{DELCODE} is a single-site cohort with a highly
regular inter-visit interval, over ninety percent of recorded intervals falling at twelve
months, described in \autoref{sec:comparability}. \ac{ADNI} spans multiple acquisition
protocols and field strengths across sites and phases, and its inter-visit interval varies
considerably more than \ac{DELCODE}'s does. A model whose temporal-first design is
motivated in part by conditioning on the interval between visits, described in
\autoref{sec:tfgn}, has correspondingly little variation to condition on within its primary
development cohort; \autoref{sec:ablations-not-run} records that a dedicated ablation of
this input was not re-reported for this reason, and that a cohort with more irregular
spacing would be needed to isolate the interval input's contribution directly. Development
on a single, temporally regular site is therefore a limitation not only to how well a result
transfers to a more heterogeneous cohort, addressed in \autoref{sec:cohort-dependence}, but
to whether a design decision motivated by temporal irregularity has been exercised by the
cohort it was tuned on at all.

\paragraph{The limits of a negative result.} Several results in \autoref{ch:results} are
negative or inconclusive by construction, and each carries a specific limitation on what it
licenses. The sequence-length probe of \autoref{sec:rq1} confounds two effects that a single
comparison cannot separate: restricting the selected configuration's own predictions to
subjects with three or more visits raises its score, while training a separate configuration
on only that restricted subject pool lowers it, so the result establishes that the loss from
a smaller training pool exceeds the gain from longer visit histories at this cohort size,
not that longer visit histories carry no additional signal. The pooling-order contrast
under matched self-supervised pretraining, discussed in \autoref{sec:ssl-contribution},
remains untested rather than resolved, because the configuration on one side of that
contrast failed to train usefully; the question it was designed to answer accordingly
remains open. The comparison against the published competitor characterised in
\autoref{sec:braintokengt} is bounded by its own run-to-run scatter and by an unequal tuning
budget relative to the architectures introduced in this thesis, both described in
\autoref{sec:comparability}; neither axis is resolved by the results reported here, and both
limit how much weight that comparison alone can carry.

\paragraph{Two further limitations.} The reproducibility split reported in
\autoref{sec:reproducibility-audit} is itself a validity consideration for any comparison
that relies on it: the temporal-first implementation evaluated in this thesis reproduces to
the last recorded digit under a fixed seed, while the published competitor's same-seed
runs exhibit run-to-run variation comparable in magnitude to the performance margins
distinguishing several configurations in \autoref{sec:rq3}. This scatter is consistent with
unengaged global determinism in scatter-gather operations inside the competitor's own
released implementation, stated as a plausible mechanism rather than as a demonstrated one,
since no experiment here holds the scheduling fixed while varying the algorithm path. Finally, the attrition reported in
\autoref{tab:attrition} shows that the two external cohorts do not lose sessions to motion
quality control at comparable rates: \ac{ADNI} loses a modest fraction of its sessions at
this stage, while \ac{OASIS} loses a substantially larger fraction of its already smaller
pool. Acquisition and cohort composition are therefore a plausible contributor to some of
the difficulty external evaluation poses, alongside the representation-level account of
\autoref{sec:cohort-dependence}, rather than a substitute for it.

\section{Clinical Relevance}\label{sec:clinical-relevance}

In this section we discuss what the results of \autoref{ch:results} say about the clinical
value of the signal this thesis studies, rather than about the readiness of any evaluated
configuration for clinical deployment; \autoref{sec:cohort-dependence} and
\autoref{sec:limitations} already state the transfer and sample-size limits that stand between
these results and a deployment claim, and this section does not restate them.

The floor comparison of \autoref{sec:rq1} shows that essentially all of the signal
available to the selected configuration is longitudinal: the same configuration evaluated
on a single visit scores at chance, while its full-trajectory evaluation reaches an
\ac{AUC} of 0.7488. A single scan, by this evidence, does not separate a subject whose
connectivity pattern is mildly abnormal but stable from one whose pattern is mildly
abnormal and declining; the second scan is what supplies the information a single
assessment cannot. Every configuration's static single-visit probe in \autoref{sec:rq1} and
\autoref{sec:rq3} lands at or below the demographics-only floor, so this is not a property
of the selected architecture alone but a property of what a single functional-connectivity
snapshot carries about future conversion at this cohort size.

The linear floor of \autoref{sec:rq1}, a logistic regression fitted directly on the
connectivity-change matrix with no learned representation, already recovers 0.7053 of the
selected configuration's 0.7488, leaving a difference of 0.0435 for every nonlinear or
learned component evaluated in this thesis to be credited with. The clinically relevant
quantity, by this comparison, is closer to the change in connectivity between visits itself
than to any particular learned representation of that change; a linear read of that
quantity already captures most of what a trained classifier adds. This bounds what claim of
added value a more complex representation can support, without denying that the remaining
margin is real.

The disease axis and per-scan disease score of \autoref{sec:interpretability-results} show a
different kind of clinical value: not a larger effect than the classification result itself,
but a more interpretable form of the same information. Projecting the frozen spatial-first
encoder's embeddings onto a single fitted axis separates converters from stable subjects
with a large effect size, and a logistic probe on the same embeddings reaches an
out-of-fold \ac{AUC} comparable to the classification results elsewhere in this thesis. The
Fisher discriminant ratio computed per latent dimension shows that this separation is
concentrated in a minority of the sixty-four latent dimensions rather than distributed
evenly across all of them, which is the practical distinction behind treating this axis as
a direction with content rather than as an artefact of the classifier's decision boundary.
Compressing a classification decision into one scalar per scan, comparable in form to a
quantitative biomarker readout, is the form of clinical value this instrumentation offers,
independently of whether the underlying classifier's \ac{AUC} itself exceeds any particular
threshold.

The perturbation results of \autoref{sec:robustness-results} add a further constraint on
what this signal reflects. Cohort decisions computed from the reconstruction-error baseline
are completely insensitive to demographic-conditioning noise, which alters only age and sex
while leaving the connectivity graph unchanged, and to edge-topology perturbation, while
remaining sensitive to direct feature-level noise at the highest level tested. This
asymmetry indicates that the decisions this instrumentation studies are driven by
graph-derived connectivity signal rather than by demographic covariates riding alongside
it, which is a precondition for treating the disease axis above as a connectivity-based
biomarker candidate rather than as a proxy for age or sex.

Taken together, these results support the value of longitudinal functional-connectivity
signal, and of a linear or near-linear summary of its change over time, as a research
instrument for locating where conversion-relevant information resides in this modality.
They do not, on their own, support a claim of clinical deployment readiness: the cohort
sizes evaluated here, the sample-size floor of \autoref{sec:limitations} and the transfer result
of \autoref{sec:cohort-dependence} are what would need to change before a claim of that kind
could be made. We accordingly restrict the claims of this section to what the evaluated
protocol licenses, in keeping with the scope stated in \autoref{sec:scope-of-claims}, rather
than placing the figures reported in this thesis alongside conversion-prediction accuracies
reported elsewhere, which were obtained under different cohorts, protocols and evaluation
practices that this thesis has not audited.
