\chapter{Data, Cohorts and Preprocessing}\label{ch:data}

\section{The DELCODE Cohort and the Conversion Label}\label{sec:delcode}

\ac{DELCODE} is the primary cohort of this thesis. Its subject pool, visit structure and
conversion label were built as part of the pre-existing project infrastructure this thesis
consumes; this section describes that cohort and label as they enter the downstream
classifiers, states the resulting cohort composition, and states explicitly what the
absence of an imposed follow-up window means for the label.

\paragraph{Diagnostic categories and cohort composition.} Every \ac{DELCODE} visit carries
one of four diagnostic categories: cognitively healthy, subjective cognitive decline,
\ac{MCI}, or \ac{AD}. A fifth group, first-degree relatives of \ac{AD} patients recruited
as an additional comparison group, is excluded from every cohort used in this thesis, since
the modelling task is restricted to the \ac{MCI}-to-\ac{AD} conversion question. Every
\ac{DELCODE} visit is additionally recorded against a nominal study timepoint on a
6-month-to-annual grid running from baseline to 60 months, used to label visits
consistently across subjects rather than to bound how long any subject is followed.

\paragraph{The conversion label.} A subject's conversion status is not read from the
per-visit diagnostic category directly. Instead, it is established by matching each visit
against a separately maintained, hand-curated record of confirmed conversions, keyed by
subject identifier and visit date. Every visit occurring after a subject's earliest
matched conversion is then relabelled \ac{AD} regardless of what diagnostic category it
originally carried, on the reasoning that a subject confirmed to have converted has, by
definition, remained converted at every later visit. A subject is a converter if any of
their visits is confirmed this way, and stable \ac{MCI} if they have at least one \ac{MCI}
visit and no confirmed conversion. Reading the label from this separately curated record,
rather than from the routinely recorded diagnostic category alone, is what lets the label
reflect a clinical confirmation step rather than a single visit's coding.

\paragraph{No maximum follow-up horizon.} The label pipeline imposes no cutoff on how long
after a subject's first \ac{MCI} visit a later conversion is still counted: a conversion
recorded at any distance in the future still promotes every subsequent visit to \ac{AD} and
marks the subject a converter. This is one endpoint of the tradeoff between cohort size and
label noise that a follow-up window controls. Choosing no maximum horizon maximises the
information used to assign every subject's label, since no confirmed conversion is ever
discarded for occurring too late to count, but it means the stable-\ac{MCI} label carries a
different meaning depending on how long a given subject happened to be followed: a subject
who stops attending study visits after twelve months without converting is labelled stable
in exactly the same way as a subject followed and observed not to convert for five years,
even though only the second subject's stability has been checked at any length. This
distinction between an unlimited-horizon label and a short one is separate from the visit
grid's own resolution: because \ac{DELCODE} visits recur on an approximately annual cadence
(\autoref{sec:sampling}), a conversion is in practice detectable only at the granularity of
whichever nominal timepoint the confirming visit falls on, not at the resolution of the
date on which conversion clinically occurred.

\paragraph{Worked example.} Consider a subject scanned at baseline, twelve and twenty-four
months, diagnosed \ac{MCI} at all three visits by the routinely recorded diagnostic
category, whose twenty-four-month visit is later matched against the curated conversion
record. Under the label pipeline, that twenty-four-month visit is relabelled \ac{AD}, and
the subject is a converter regardless of what the raw diagnostic category at that visit
said. Had the same subject instead stopped attending after the twelve-month visit, before
any conversion record could exist for them, the pipeline would label them stable \ac{MCI}
from exactly the same clinical trajectory up to that point, since no later visit was
observed at which a conversion could be confirmed. The two outcomes differ only in how long
the subject happened to remain in the study, which is the label-noise mechanism the
unlimited-horizon choice trades against a larger, more fully labelled cohort.

\paragraph{Downstream cohort.} Restricting to subjects with a valid stable-\ac{MCI} or
converter label under this rule gives a downstream cohort of 167 subjects: 99 stable
\ac{MCI} and 68 converters. This cohort is split at the subject level into training,
validation and test sets in a 60/20/20 ratio, stratified by diagnostic group and fixed at
seed 42, so that every visit belonging to a given subject falls in exactly one split
(\autoref{sec:leakage}).

\section{Longitudinal Sampling and Follow-Up Regularity}\label{sec:sampling}

Longitudinal classifiers condition on how a subject's connectivity changes between visits,
which makes the spacing and number of those visits part of the input rather than incidental
detail. This section describes how visit timing is represented for each cohort, how a
variable number of visits per subject is handled, and how regular or irregular that visit
spacing actually is in \ac{DELCODE} against \ac{ADNI} and \ac{OASIS}.

\paragraph{Three notions of visit time.} A subject's visits are indexed three separate
ways, kept distinct because they answer different questions. The visit index is a subject's
visits numbered in acquisition order, independent of any calendar or protocol time, and is
what a sequence model iterates over. The protocol month is the nominal study timepoint a
visit is recorded against, meaningful for \ac{DELCODE}, whose visits are scheduled on the
fixed grid described in \autoref{sec:delcode}, but not for \ac{ADNI} or \ac{OASIS}, which
instead record each visit's elapsed time in days since a cohort-specific reference date.
The inter-visit interval, $\Delta t$, is the elapsed time between two consecutive visits,
converted to months by dividing the day difference by $30.44$, the average number of days
per month, and is what the recurrent trajectory models of \autoref{ch:methodology}
condition their gate updates on. Keeping these three separate matters because a fixed
protocol month is not itself an elapsed time: two \ac{DELCODE} subjects both recorded at
the twelve-month timepoint may have been scanned weeks apart in practice, and treating the
nominal grid as if it were the elapsed time would misstate $\Delta t$ for exactly the
subjects whose visits fall off-schedule.

\paragraph{A variable number of visits per subject.} Subjects are not observed the same
number of times. A sequence dataset built from these visits enforces a minimum visit count
before a subject is included at all, and optionally truncates every kept subject to a fixed
maximum, retaining that subject's earliest visits. A stricter setting requires every kept
subject to be truncated to exactly this maximum, and raises an error if no maximum is given
under this setting, since without a fixed length there is nothing to truncate every subject
to.

\paragraph{Visit count as a label proxy.} In the \ac{DELCODE} downstream cohort, converters
are followed for more visits, on average, than stable \ac{MCI} subjects: in the held-out
test split, converters average 3.71 scans per subject against 2.35 for stable \ac{MCI}
subjects. Subjects who stop attending study visits earlier are therefore disproportionately
stable \ac{MCI}, so the number of visits available for a subject is not independent of that
subject's label. This is why the stricter fixed-length truncation setting exists: without
it, a sequence model has access to a subject's visit count as a side channel correlated
with the label it is asked to predict, alongside whatever signal the visits' content
carries.

\paragraph{Follow-up regularity.} \autoref{tab:interval-structure} reports each cohort's
visit spacing, summarised by the coefficient of variation
$\mathrm{CV} = \sigma_{\Delta t} / \mu_{\Delta t}$, the ratio of the standard deviation of a
cohort's inter-visit intervals to their mean, which is near zero for a cohort whose visits
recur at a near-fixed spacing and grows as that spacing becomes less predictable relative
to its own average length. \ac{DELCODE} visits fall on the twelve-month nominal timepoint
90.0\% of the time, which is why no coefficient of variation is reported for it: its
follow-up is regular enough that the irregular-sampling literature's usual concerns, built
around cohorts like \ac{ADNI} and \ac{OASIS} whose visit spacing this table quantifies,
apply only weakly to \ac{DELCODE} on its own. \ac{ADNI} and \ac{OASIS} are markedly less
regular: \ac{ADNI}'s median inter-visit interval is 371 days (interquartile range
207 to 419 days, $\mathrm{CV} = 0.647$), with only 21.9\% of intervals falling near the
twelve-month mark, and \ac{OASIS}'s median interval is 1{,}012 days (interquartile range
708 to 1{,}342 days, $\mathrm{CV} = 0.574$), with its most common interval, at 8.1\% of
cases, falling near thirty-six months rather than twelve. \autoref{sec:pooled-protocol}
records that pooling \ac{ADNI} into the \ac{DELCODE}-based training pool reintroduces this
irregularity into a cohort that would otherwise be sampled on a near-fixed schedule.

Within \ac{DELCODE} itself, the distribution of inter-visit intervals concentrates sharply
on the twelve-month nominal spacing: 242 of 269 recorded intervals (90.0\%) fall at twelve
months, 19 (7.1\%) at twenty-four months, 7 (2.6\%) at thirty-six months and 1 (0.4\%) at
forty-eight months. Because this near-annual cadence is also the resolution at which
\autoref{sec:delcode}'s conversion label is detectable, \ac{DELCODE}'s follow-up regularity
and its label resolution are two views of the same scheduled grid, rather than independent
properties of the cohort.

\section{External Cohorts: ADNI and OASIS-3}\label{sec:external-cohorts}

As part of the broader project, \ac{ADNI} and \ac{OASIS} were included as additional
cohorts for cross-cohort validation. During the thesis, these datasets were processed and
integrated into the existing analysis framework. This section describes the acquisition
heterogeneity of both cohorts relative to \ac{DELCODE}, the diagnostic-label conventions
used to identify conversion in each, and a scan-to-visit matching problem specific to
multi-site data that does not arise in \ac{DELCODE}.

\ac{DELCODE} is acquired at a single site under one nominal protocol
(\autoref{sec:preprocessing} gives the exact scanning parameters), so within-cohort
acquisition variability is not a concern for it. \ac{ADNI} and \ac{OASIS}, by contrast,
pool scans collected over more than a decade across many sites, scanner vendors and
hardware generations, which is the acquisition-heterogeneity challenge introduced in
\autoref{ch:foundations}. An internal inventory of the \ac{ADNI} magnetic-resonance record
metadata used for this project counts 18,616 records, of which 18,614 are acquired at
3.0\,T field strength and only 2 at 1.5\,T, drawn from at least ten scanner models across
three vendors (Siemens, 14,056 records; Philips, 2,416; General Electric, 2,144). \ac{OASIS}
draws on a single vendor, Siemens, but spans six scanner generations over its acquisition
window; its record inventory totals 30,339 magnetic-resonance sessions, of which 5,114
scan rows across 2,138 sessions and 1,198 subjects are resting-state functional scans. Of
these, 205 rows are reference or calibration acquisitions (\texttt{SeriesDescription} of
\texttt{rsfmri\_ref}, repetition time 5.0\,s) rather than usable resting runs and are
excluded before any downstream matching.

\paragraph{Repetition-time grouping and a unit trap.} Cohort-level protocol comparisons
group scans by repetition time (below 1{,}000\,ms as one group, below 2{,}000\,ms as a
second, and 2{,}000\,ms and above as a third). \ac{OASIS} stores repetition and echo time
in seconds in its scan metadata (for example 2.2\,s, 0.027\,s), whereas \ac{ADNI} stores
the same fields directly in milliseconds; applying the grouping thresholds to \ac{OASIS}
without first multiplying by 1{,}000 places every scan in the wrong group. Once converted,
\ac{OASIS} resting-state scans populate only two of the three repetition-time groups: one
at 2{,}200, 2{,}500 or 3{,}000\,ms and a second at 500, 806 or 816\,ms; the middle group is
empty. This unit conversion was implemented as part of the cohort-integration work carried
out during the thesis, alongside the rest of the \ac{OASIS} subject-list construction
described below.

\paragraph{An era boundary in the diagnostic label.} \ac{ADNI} and \ac{OASIS} each label a
subject a converter from the same rule: a subject with at least one visit diagnosed
\ac{MCI} is a converter if any later visit is diagnosed \ac{AD}, and stable \ac{MCI}
otherwise; a subject whose only \ac{AD} diagnosis occurs at or before the first \ac{MCI}
visit is treated as prevalent \ac{AD} rather than a converter and is dropped, since the
label is meant to be predicted ahead of conversion rather than recognised in visits that
are already demented.
Applying this rule to \ac{OASIS} exposes a form-schema change that a same-cohort analysis
would not: \ac{OASIS} diagnoses are drawn from the Uniform Data Set clinical form, whose
Alzheimer's-disease field was renamed partway through the study. Earlier visits carry a
single field, \texttt{PROBAD}; later visits instead carry two fields, \texttt{alzdis} and
\texttt{alzdisif}, and leave \texttt{PROBAD} unset. Of 8{,}499 diagnostic rows, 4{,}855
belong to the earlier form era and 3{,}644 to the later one, and the two eras are
essentially non-overlapping: \texttt{PROBAD} is populated in 1{,}131 of the 4{,}855 earlier
rows and in none of the 3{,}644 later rows. A subject is therefore labelled \ac{AD} at a
visit if
\[
  \text{DEMENTED} = 1 \;\wedge\; \bigl(\text{PROBAD} = 1 \;\vee\; (\text{alzdis} = 1 \wedge \text{alzdisif} = 1)\bigr),
\]
where $\text{DEMENTED}$ is the form's general dementia flag, $\text{PROBAD}$ is the
earlier-era probable-\ac{AD} flag, and $\text{alzdis}$ together with $\text{alzdisif}$ are
the later-era Alzheimer's-disease and Alzheimer's-as-primary-cause flags that replace it.
Using $\text{PROBAD}$ alone, ignoring the later form era, yields 23 converters and 130
stable \ac{MCI} subjects; the era-aware rule above yields 73 converters and 85 stable
\ac{MCI} subjects, nearly tripling the converter count. The single-field rule under-counts
because it silently treats every later-era \ac{AD} visit as non-\ac{AD}, which is exactly
the failure mode that motivates checking a label rule against both form eras of a cohort
before trusting its counts, rather than assuming a diagnostic field keeps the same meaning
across a multi-year, multi-site study.

\paragraph{Matching a scan to a clinical visit.} Neither cohort scans a subject on the same
day as its clinical assessment, so a scan is assigned to the nearest visit within a fixed
window and left unmatched otherwise. \ac{ADNI} uses a window of $\pm 90$ days; \ac{OASIS}
uses $\pm 365$ days. The difference follows from visit cadence rather than from a different
tolerance for label noise: \ac{OASIS} clinical visits recur at approximately annual
intervals, so a $\pm 90$-day window matches only 33 visit-scan pairs across the cohort,
against 122 matched visit-scans over 84 subjects at $\pm 365$ days. A fixed matching window
carried over unchanged from \ac{ADNI} to \ac{OASIS} would therefore have discarded most of
the cohort's imaging data before any modelling step saw it.

\paragraph{No calendar dates in \ac{OASIS}.} \ac{ADNI} visit and scan records carry an
actual calendar exam date. \ac{OASIS}, being fully de-identified, carries no calendar date
anywhere in its metadata: every visit and scan is instead timestamped as an integer count
of days since an arbitrary per-subject entry point. The two cohorts' visit and scan
timestamp columns are given identical names for downstream compatibility, but the
\ac{OASIS} columns hold day offsets rather than dates, and parsing them as dates produces
meaningless values. This is a cohort-integration detail specific to processing \ac{OASIS},
addressed as part of the thesis when the \ac{OASIS} subject-list construction was built to
mirror the pre-existing \ac{ADNI} pipeline.

Applying the classification rule above to \ac{OASIS} yields 73 converters and 85 stable
\ac{MCI} subjects; 67 of the converters and 73 of the stable \ac{MCI} subjects have at
least one scan matched within the $\pm 365$-day window, for 122 matched visit-scans across
84 subjects in total. The corresponding \ac{ADNI} classification, built on the same rule
and applied to a substantially larger record inventory, is carried through the further
attrition and split-construction stages described in \autoref{sec:pooled-protocol} and
\autoref{sec:preprocessing}.

\section{The Pooled ADNI and DELCODE Protocol}\label{sec:pooled-protocol}

The pooled protocol implemented during the thesis builds on the pre-existing
\ac{DELCODE} split definitions and leakage-control framework, extending these structures
to the additional cohort. This section describes how the two cohorts' independently built
splits are combined into one training and evaluation protocol, the schema that keeps their
cohort-specific timing conventions apart while sharing a single dataset interface, and the
resulting split sizes.

\ac{ADNI} enters the pooled protocol with a downstream split of 192 subjects (115 train,
38 validation, 39 test; 65 converters, 33.9\% of the cohort) that was already stratified
and seeded before pooling. \ac{DELCODE} contributes its own downstream split of 167
subjects (99 stable \ac{MCI}, 68 converters; 99 train, 34 validation, 34 test subjects,
covering 308, 100 and 99 scans respectively) together with a separate pretraining split of
241, 115 and 116 subjects (371, 217 and 224 scans) used for the self-supervised encoder.
\ac{ADNI} had no pretraining split of its own; one was built for it during the thesis by
re-applying \ac{DELCODE}'s own two-stage splitting procedure and seed policy to \ac{ADNI},
so that the pretraining data for both cohorts follow one leakage-control rule rather than
two independently designed ones.

Pooling applies a minimum-visit filter (two visits per subject by default) to every cohort
before combining them, because the downstream classifiers described in
\autoref{sec:sampling} require at least one inter-visit interval to condition on. This
filter removes no \ac{ADNI} subjects: its 192-subject downstream split already satisfies
the two-visit minimum in full, so its pooled contribution is unchanged at 153 subjects
across the pooled training and validation splits and 39 in the pooled test split.
\ac{DELCODE}, whose downstream split includes single-visit subjects, shrinks under the
same filter from 167 to 120 subjects: 95 across the pooled training and validation splits
and 25 in the pooled test split. \autoref{tab:pooled-splits} gives the resulting pooled
split sizes.

\ac{OASIS} is excluded from every upstream stage, including self-supervised pretraining,
so that it remains a genuinely external test rather than a held-out fold of data the
pipeline has otherwise seen. Every pooled split uses the same four seeds
(42, 43, 44, 45) as the single-cohort splits it is built from.

\paragraph{Keeping cohort-specific timing apart.} \ac{DELCODE} conditions its trajectory
models on nominal protocol months and \ac{ADNI} and \ac{OASIS} on elapsed calendar days
(\autoref{sec:sampling}), and the pooling step preserves this distinction rather than
forcing every cohort onto one time axis. One subject-level dataset object is built per
cohort, each pointed at that cohort's own data root and its own time-allow-list column,
and the resulting per-cohort datasets are concatenated rather than merged into a shared
table. The non-native timing column is dropped from each cohort's dataset before
concatenation, and construction raises an error if the retained column is entirely empty
for a given cohort. Without that check, a dataset implementation that falls back to
whichever timing column is populated first would silently disable \ac{DELCODE}'s
post-conversion visit exclusion (\autoref{sec:delcode}) for every pooled subject, which is
exactly the kind of leakage-relevant bug rule \autoref{sec:leakage} is written to catch
before it reaches a checkpoint.

\paragraph{A schema-level guard on the same principle.} The two cohorts' allow-list columns
are named \texttt{allowed\_months} and \texttt{allowed\_days} and are mutually exclusive by
construction: the asset-building script raises an error if both are populated on the same
row, because a row carrying both would leave the leakage filter ambiguous about which
column governs it. The same script materialises a symlink farm over both cohorts'
functional-connectivity matrices so that a single glob root can serve the pooled dataset
without duplicating any file, and refuses to overwrite an existing symlink that does not
already point at the expected source file, rather than silently repointing it.

\paragraph{Disjointness and containment.} Three groups of assertions enforce
\autoref{sec:leakage}'s split-integrity contract across the pooling step itself. Within the
pooled pretraining split, the training, validation and test subject sets are pairwise
disjoint; within the pooled downstream split, the same three sets are pairwise disjoint;
and across the two, the pooled pretraining training set is disjoint from the pooled
downstream validation and test sets, while the pooled downstream validation and test sets
are each required to be subsets of their pretraining-split counterparts, so that no subject
used to validate or test the downstream classifier can also have contributed to training
the upstream encoder. \autoref{sec:leakage} discusses why the equivalent check failed to
generalise to the pooled protocol on its first implementation.

Because the two-visit minimum removes DELCODE's single-visit subjects but not \ac{ADNI}'s,
pooling reintroduces some of the follow-up irregularity that \autoref{sec:sampling}
records \ac{DELCODE} alone as largely free of: the pooled cross-validation pool inherits
\ac{ADNI}'s wider and less regular inter-visit intervals alongside \ac{DELCODE}'s
near-annual ones.

\section{Preprocessing and Confound Management}\label{sec:preprocessing}

\ac{DELCODE} had been preprocessed before the start of this thesis using the laboratory's
existing preprocessing pipeline. During the thesis, the same established preprocessing
framework was applied to \ac{ADNI} and \ac{OASIS}. Cohort-specific adaptations required for
processing and integrating these datasets were implemented as part of the thesis. This
section describes that pipeline end to end, from the raw scan to the parcellated,
confound-corrected connectivity matrix each downstream model consumes, and reports how many
sessions each cohort loses at every stage.

\paragraph{Acquisition.} \ac{DELCODE} is acquired at a single site under one nominal
resting-state protocol: a repetition time of 2{,}580\,ms, an echo time of 30\,ms, 3.5\,mm
isotropic voxels and 180 acquired volumes per scan, on a single scanner model. \ac{ADNI}
and \ac{OASIS} are acquired across the many sites, vendors and scanner generations
described in \autoref{sec:external-cohorts}. This single-site homogeneity is what makes
\ac{DELCODE} the appropriate cohort for the group-level statistics of
\autoref{sec:group-stats}: the acquisition variability that has to be explicitly modelled
or excluded for \ac{ADNI} and \ac{OASIS} is largely absent from \ac{DELCODE} by
construction.

\paragraph{From raw scan to denoised time series.} Every cohort passes through the same
pipeline. Raw scans are converted to a standardised imaging directory layout, then run
through fMRIPrep (version 20.2.7) for motion correction, susceptibility-distortion
correction, coregistration to an anatomical reference and normalisation to a common
template space, with automated independent-component-based motion labelling enabled and
FreeSurfer surface reconstruction disabled, since the downstream models operate on volume
parcellations rather than cortical surfaces. Ten volumes are dropped from the start of each
scan before any further processing, to let the scanner's signal reach steady state. The
labelled motion components identified by fMRIPrep, together with the mean white-matter and
cerebrospinal-fluid signals and one global-signal regressor, are then regressed out of the
preprocessed time series in a single denoising call together with a band-pass filter that
retains frequencies between 0.01 and 0.1\,Hz, the conventional resting-state
functional-connectivity band. Regressing the automated motion components out of the
minimally preprocessed signal, rather than starting from a partially cleaned variant, keeps
exactly one denoising step between the raw signal and the final time series, so the effect
of each individual regressor stays traceable.

\paragraph{Motion quality control.} A session is excluded outright if its mean
frame-to-frame head displacement, computed after the ten discarded volumes, exceeds
0.5\,mm. A second, looser threshold of 0.2\,mm is computed and reported per session but
does not itself exclude a session; it is a scrub-level diagnostic rather than a gate.
Sessions excluded by the 0.5\,mm gate have a mean displacement above 0.67\,mm (maximum
1.40\,mm), with more than half of their timepoints spiking above 0.5\,mm and more than
92\% of frames above the looser 0.2\,mm threshold. The excluded sessions are therefore
sessions in which the subject was moving through most of the scan rather than borderline
cases close to the 0.5\,mm cutoff.

\paragraph{Parcellation and connectivity.} The denoised time series are averaged within
each of 200 cortical regions of the Schaefer parcellation, itself organised into seven
canonical large-scale networks, giving one region-average time series per node. Pairwise
Pearson correlation between every pair of regions' time series gives one
$200 \times 200$ connectivity matrix per session, which is then Fisher-transformed:
\[
  z_{ij} = \operatorname{arctanh}\bigl(\operatorname{clip}(r_{ij}, -0.999999, 0.999999)\bigr),
\]
where $r_{ij}$ is the Pearson correlation between regions $i$ and $j$ and
$\operatorname{clip}$ bounds it strictly inside $(-1, 1)$ before the inverse hyperbolic
tangent is applied, since $\operatorname{arctanh}$ is unbounded at $\pm 1$ and a
perfectly correlated pair of regions, which does occur in a 180-volume scan, would
otherwise produce an infinite matrix entry. The Fisher transform is applied because Pearson
correlation is bounded and its sampling variance depends on the correlation's own
magnitude, so a linear model fitted directly on $r_{ij}$ would treat a change from 0.10 to
0.20 as equivalent in magnitude to a change from 0.80 to 0.90, when the latter reflects a
much larger displacement in the underlying uncorrelated-to-fully-correlated range that
$\operatorname{arctanh}$ maps closer to linearly. A small number of hippocampus-focused
analyses extend this cortical parcellation with subcortical hippocampal parcels from the
Tian subcortical atlas; these variant matrices are used only where the analysis in question
calls for them and are not part of the whole-brain matrices the main classifiers consume.

\paragraph{Age regression.} A subject's age correlates with functional connectivity
independently of diagnosis, so every connectivity entry is additionally corrected for a
linear age effect before it reaches a classifier. For each edge $(i,j)$, a linear model
\[
  C_{ij} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \, \text{Age} + \varepsilon_{ij}
\]
is fit across the cohort, where $C_{ij}$ is that edge's Fisher-transformed connectivity
value, $\text{Age}$ is the subject's age at scan, $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ are the
intercept and age slope estimated by ordinary least squares, and $\varepsilon_{ij}$ is the
residual. Each subject's edge value is then replaced by
$C_{ij} - \hat\beta_1 (\text{Age} - \overline{\text{Age}})$, where $\hat\beta_1$ is the
fitted slope and $\overline{\text{Age}}$ is the cohort mean age, which removes the linear
age trend while leaving the cohort's overall connectivity level unchanged, since a subject
at exactly the mean age is left untouched by construction.

\paragraph{Attrition.} \autoref{tab:attrition} reports how many sessions and subjects each
external cohort loses between the standardised imaging directory and the final,
model-ready manifest. \ac{ADNI} loses 40 sessions across 31 subjects (5.6\% of its
674 postprocessed sessions) to the motion gate; \ac{OASIS} loses a larger share, 36
sessions across 23 subjects (13.3\% of 276), consistent with its higher mean motion
(0.340\,mm against \ac{ADNI}'s 0.229\,mm) once both cohorts are pooled. Of the 18 subjects
present after motion quality control but absent from the final manifest, 4 belong to
\ac{ADNI} (every session for that subject failed quality control) and 14 to \ac{OASIS} (12
with every session quality-control-failed and 2 missing the confound files the denoising
step requires).

\section{Group-Level Characterisation Before Modelling}\label{sec:group-stats}

Before any classifier is fit, this section asks whether the \ac{DELCODE} connectivity
matrices already separate by diagnostic group under classical statistics. Two separate
analyses answer this. The first, a within-group and between-group variance decomposition,
compares converters directly against pooled non-converters, the contrast the downstream
classifiers of \autoref{ch:methodology} are trained on. The second, a multivariate and
edge-level battery, applies \ac{PERMANOVA}, the \ac{NBS}, and per-edge effect sizes under
\ac{FDR} control to converters against established \ac{AD} patients, a coarser and more
clinically separable contrast used here to characterise the same connectivity matrices at
the edge and network level. Both analyses draw on the full pool of available \ac{DELCODE}
visits assigned each subject's diagnostic label at the time of that visit, rather than on
the single-visit-per-subject, subject-level cohort of \autoref{sec:delcode}, since the
question here is about group separability in the connectivity matrices themselves rather
than about the modelling cohort.

\paragraph{Converters against pooled non-converters: a variance decomposition.} For each of
five connectivity-distance metrics (Pearson correlation, Spearman and Kendall rank
correlation, cross-correlation, and a Riemannian distance between connectivity matrices
treated as covariance-like objects), every edge's variance is decomposed into a within-group
component, the average variance of that edge across visits sharing the same diagnostic
label, and a between-group component, the variance of that edge's group means around the
overall mean. The ratio of the two,
\[
  \rho = \frac{\sigma^2_{\text{between}}}{\sigma^2_{\text{within}}},
\]
is large when an edge's value differs consistently between converters and non-converters
relative to how much it varies within either group, and small when between-patient
variation swamps whatever group difference exists. Across all five metrics, the converter
group (60 visits) and the pooled non-converter group (692 to 734 visits, depending on the
metric's missing-data pattern) give a ratio between 0.0130 and 0.0157
(\autoref{tab:variance-ratio}): within-group variance exceeds between-group variance by
roughly two orders of magnitude for every metric tested, and not one of the 5{,}995
edges evaluated reaches $\rho > 1$ under any metric. Converters and non-converters are
therefore not separable at the level of individual edge variance under any of the five
distance metrics tested: between-patient variation within either diagnostic group is the
dominant source of variance in this cohort's connectivity matrices, at every edge and under
every metric considered here.

\paragraph{Converters against AD: distance-based group tests.} \ac{PERMANOVA} tests whether
two groups differ in their average pairwise distance in connectivity space by comparing an
observed pseudo-$F$ statistic against a null distribution built by permuting group labels:
\[
  F = \frac{\mathrm{SS}_{\text{between}} / (g - 1)}{\mathrm{SS}_{\text{within}} / (N - g)},
\]
where $\mathrm{SS}_{\text{between}}$ and $\mathrm{SS}_{\text{within}}$ are the between-group
and within-group sums of squared pairwise distances computed from the same distance matrix
each metric induces, $g$ is the number of groups compared, and $N$ is the total number of
subjects. Across 5{,}000 label permutations per metric, none of the five distance metrics
yields a significant \ac{PERMANOVA} result at $\alpha = 0.05$ for converters against
\ac{AD} (\autoref{tab:permanova-nbs}). The \ac{NBS} instead tests for group differences
concentrated in a connected subnetwork of edges rather than distributed diffusely: a
per-edge Welch $t$-statistic is computed for every edge, edges with $|t| > 3.0$ are kept and
treated as a graph, and the size of each connected component in that graph is compared
against the largest component size obtained under 5{,}000 label permutations, giving each
observed component a permutation-based $p$-value. Four of the five metrics again show no
significant component at $\alpha = 0.05$; the cross-correlation metric is the exception, with
a 24-edge connected component reaching $p = 0.024$.

Per-edge effect sizes are computed alongside these tests with a Mann-Whitney $U$ test at
every edge, corrected for the resulting multiple comparisons at $\alpha = 0.05$ under the
Benjamini-Hochberg procedure for controlling the \ac{FDR}, and paired with Cohen's $d$ as
the effect-size statistic at each edge. The near-uniform absence of a significant group
difference across four of five metrics, for a contrast as clinically separable as converters
against established \ac{AD}, sets the scale against which the pooled non-converter
comparison above should be read: if converters are not consistently distinguishable from
manifest \ac{AD} patients under these edge-level and network-level tests, the smaller,
within-cohort-variance-dominated difference between converters and stable \ac{MCI} subjects
is not expected to be more detectable by the same methods.

\section{Split Integrity and Leakage Control}\label{sec:leakage}

Every split described in this chapter, single-cohort and pooled alike, is built at the
subject level and checked against the same integrity contract before it is used for
training. This section states that contract, describes the automated audit and test suite
that enforce it, and reports a historical violation of it that motivated part of the
contract in its current form.

\paragraph{Patient-level splits and the isolated test manifest.} Every visit belonging to a
given subject is assigned to exactly one of a training, validation or test split, never
split across them, which is what makes the split patient-level rather than visit-level.
Applied naively, a visit-level split would let two visits from the same subject fall on
opposite sides of a split boundary, so that a model evaluated on one of them had already
seen a near-identical scan from the same subject during training; the patient-level rule
rules this out by construction. The resulting test manifest is materialised once per cohort
and is not regenerated or resampled between experiments, so that every model compared
against it in \autoref{ch:results} is evaluated against the same held-out subjects.

\paragraph{Post-conversion visit exclusion.} A converter's visits after their confirmed
conversion date carry the relabelled \ac{AD} diagnosis described in
\autoref{sec:delcode}; these visits are excluded from the pool of visits a downstream
classifier is trained or evaluated to predict conversion from, since a visit already coded
\ac{AD} is no longer an instance of the prediction problem the label is meant to represent.
This exclusion is enforced through the same allow-list columns introduced in
\autoref{sec:pooled-protocol}: a subject's allowed visits are exactly those up to and
including their first confirmed conversion, and a test asserts directly that no
post-conversion visit ever appears in a converter's allowed set.

\paragraph{Validation-derived thresholds.} No decision threshold used to score a classifier
is derived from the test split itself; \autoref{ch:methodology} fixes the exact
threshold-selection rule used throughout. Stating this here, alongside the split contract,
keeps the leakage-control framing in one place: a threshold chosen on the same data it is
then used to score is a narrower version of the same problem a visit-level split creates,
information from evaluation data reaching a decision the evaluation is meant to be blind
to.

\paragraph{The executable audit.} A shared auditing module provides four checks, each
raising an error naming the specific violation rather than returning a pass/fail flag
silently. One checks that a named collection of split files is pairwise disjoint at the
subject level, raising with the offending pair of splits, the number of shared subjects and
example subject identifiers. A second hashes every connectivity matrix referenced by a
split and raises if two distinct file paths hash identically, catching a matrix
accidentally duplicated across subjects or visits. A third re-runs the stratified,
group-aware cross-validation fold assignment and asserts that no subject appears in more
than one fold's validation set. A fourth checks whether a self-supervised pretraining
cohort and a downstream cohort satisfy a stated containment policy, either that they must be
disjoint or that overlap is permitted, and is the check that directly targets the
containment failure described below. A single audit entry point runs a subset of these
checks, currently only the pairwise-disjointness check, against whichever split dictionary
it is called with; it is not yet a maintained default that always chains all four checks
together, which is a limitation of the automated audit as it currently stands and is stated
here rather than left implicit, since assuming the entry point runs every check would
overstate how much protection calling it actually provides.

\paragraph{A historical containment failure.} A prior split-generation script reserved the
downstream classifier's \emph{test} subjects out of the self-supervised encoder's training
pool, but not its \emph{validation} subjects, which went undetected until a supervisor
observed an unexplained 0.10 to 0.15 drop in downstream test \ac{AUC} between an older and
a newer classifier run and asked why. The investigation found that 69 of 106 subjects
(65\%) in the downstream validation split had also been used to train the encoder. A
controlled re-run, holding the encoder architecture and hyperparameters fixed and varying
only whether the leaking split or a corrected, fully disjoint split was used, isolated the
leak's own contribution: test \ac{AUC} was 0.9667 under the original encoder and the
leaking split, 0.8929 under the same encoder and hyperparameters but the corrected split,
and 0.8071 under the newer encoder, newer hyperparameters and the corrected split. Of the
full 0.1596-point drop between the original and newer runs, the leak alone, isolated by
this re-run, accounts for 0.074 points, or roughly 46\%; the remaining 54\% is attributable
to the encoder and hyperparameter changes made in the same refactor, which the leak
investigation cannot separate further because both changed together. This is the case that
motivated the pretrain-versus-downstream containment check described above, and the
pooled-protocol assertions of \autoref{sec:pooled-protocol} extend the same check to the
pooled splits so that pooling \ac{ADNI} into the training pool cannot reopen it.

\paragraph{An executable test suite.} Beyond the audit module, a dedicated test suite pins
27 invariants against every committed split file: that each split's required columns are
present and free of nulls in key fields; that a converter's post-conversion visits never
appear in their allowed set; that every split is internally free of duplicate rows; that
splits are pairwise disjoint within the pretraining schema and within the downstream
schema; that no subject is retained with zero usable scans; that diagnostic categories
outside the modelled scope, such as subjective cognitive decline, never appear in either
schema; that class balance across splits stays within a stated tolerance; and, as the direct
descendants of the containment failure above, that the pretraining training set is disjoint
from the downstream validation and test sets, that the pretraining validation set is
disjoint from the downstream test set, and that the downstream validation and test sets are
each fully contained in their pretraining-split counterparts. \autoref{ch:app-splits}
records the full list of checks this test suite and the audit module pin, together with the
audit module's exact function signatures, as a specification of the contract rather than as
a live execution log, since the split and manifest files this audit runs against are
generated artefacts rather than files committed to the project repository.
